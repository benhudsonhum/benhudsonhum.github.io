The challenge

Broad goals around girls' education, life skills, participation and safeguarding had to become coherent learning that facilitators could prepare, deliver and adapt. The work also had to function in settings with different group sizes, literacy levels, time constraints and available materials.

The Life Skills manual is one concrete anchor within a longer body of work that connected needs analysis, curriculum architecture, training, delivery support, assessment and revision.

My role

As Curriculum, Training and Assessment Advisor, I led curriculum development within the wider Theatre for a Change program team. I analysed organizational and program needs, developed learning outcomes and architecture, designed participatory activities, and produced complete manuals, facilitator guidance and learner resources.

I later returned as a freelance instructional designer and curriculum developer. That work included girls' education and life-skills programs involving the UK FCDO and Malawi's Ministry of Education, as well as GIZ-supported take-home and self-study materials for sensitive and emergency contexts.

Design approach

Design the whole path from need to delivery.

I developed an organizational ADDIE-based curriculum process connecting analysis, learning outcomes, curriculum architecture, participatory activity design, resource development, facilitator preparation, implementation and evaluation. The process made revision part of delivery rather than a final editorial step.

The 90-activity manual applied that logic at session level. Each activity had to help the facilitator understand its purpose, prepare the materials, guide participation, observe practice and lead a useful debrief.

Key design decisions

Judge the curriculum by what can happen in the room.

1. Design for the facilitator's next action

Make purpose, preparation, delivery and debrief usable without requiring facilitators to reconstruct the designer's intent.

2. Make participation the learning mechanism

Use discussion, rehearsal, role-play, problem solving and reflection instead of relying on explanation alone.

3. Balance consistency with adaptation

Keep the learning purpose and sequence stable while offering practical options for context, literacy, time and materials.

4. Connect activities into progression

Build 90 activities into a usable learning resource rather than an unstructured collection of exercises.

Facilitation in practice: Eswatini

Three-week facilitator-development cycle

I designed curriculum for a three-week facilitator program and delivered it for 20 youth peer educators in an international partner context in Eswatini.

Across three weeks, participants experienced the methods, rehearsed facilitation, received feedback and prepared to deliver the curriculum themselves.

Testing and evaluation

Evaluation began with needs and delivery constraints, then continued through facilitation. I observed where instructions, pacing, materials or support caused difficulty and gathered feedback from facilitators and stakeholders.

That evidence informed revisions to instructions, sequencing, visual resources and facilitator guidance. Assessment and evaluation guided improvements to implementation. The evidence shown here documents the resulting materials and delivery process.

What the work produced

  • An organizational ADDIE-based curriculum-development process
  • A Life Skills manual with 90 classroom-ready participatory activities
  • Complete manuals and facilitator guidance
  • Girls' education and life-skills program resources
  • Take-home, self-study and original visual resources
  • Facilitator training, implementation support, assessment and revision

What I learned

This work taught me to judge curriculum by what a facilitator can actually do with it. A sound objective is not enough. The activity, instructions, pacing, materials and debrief all have to survive contact with a real room, a real group and the constraints of delivery.
