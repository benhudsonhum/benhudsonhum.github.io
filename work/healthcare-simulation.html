CHEO Research Institute · Healthcare professional education

ADHD diagnostic-interview training across live and self-directed formats

I led instructional design in partnership with clinical experts, who reviewed and approved the clinical content. Together, we translated a complex diagnostic-interview task into guided live practice and a self-directed online course for healthcare professionals.

The challenge

The learning had to support clinical judgement rather than simple recall. Healthcare professionals needed to notice relevant evidence, apply criteria consistently and make decisions across interviews that did not present in identical ways.

The self-directed course also had to carry forward the explanation, guided practice and reasoning-focused feedback available in live training. Clinical expertise and supervision remained with the expert teams.

My role

I led the learning architecture, storyboards, scripts, interactive activities, assessment, simulation pattern and Brightspace implementation. Clinical experts retained authority for the clinical content and reviewed it throughout development.

I worked with clinicians, healthcare researchers, actors, developers and instructors to coordinate multimedia production, testing and revision. I also maintained templates, learner and facilitator resources, standards, assets and change documentation.

Formats and needs analysis

One judgement task, supported in two ways.

Instructor-led and webinar training

Live explanation and modelling introduced the task. Guided practice, facilitated actor-based simulations, feedback and discussion let learners rehearse decisions with expert support.

Self-directed online course

Organized lecture video, interactive criteria learning, examples, active checks and decision-based interview simulation carried the same reasoning into Brightspace. Feedback explained why an answer mattered, while assessment and completion logic supported progression.

This practical needs analysis combined clinical expert input, experience delivering the live training, observable interviewing tasks, recurring learner questions and errors, and the requirements of research and evaluation.

Key design decisions

Keep the judgement task visible.

1. Make judgement observable

Break the interview task into evidence and decisions rather than presenting criteria as information to memorize.

2. Use varied cases to support transfer

Design multiple actor-based cases so learners practise the same reasoning across different presentations.

3. Explain the reasoning in feedback

Show why evidence matters and how it affects a decision, not merely whether an answer is correct.

4. Treat implementation and QA as design

Build course flow, completion logic, media behaviour, assessment and documentation as part of the learning experience.

Testing and evaluation

QA covered clinical accuracy, learner comprehension, Brightspace behaviour, completion logic, assessment functionality, media, captions, accessibility and navigation. Research, learner feedback, technical QA and revision formed one improvement cycle.

I coordinated a formal non-inferiority study comparing the self-directed and instructor-led models. Because the findings are not yet published, this portfolio focuses on the design, quality assurance and evaluation processes rather than reporting results.

What the work produced

  • Instructor-led, webinar and self-directed training pathways
  • 48 actor-based video simulations
  • Interactive online learning and assessment
  • Brightspace implementation and QA
  • Facilitator and learner resources
  • Formal evaluation and evidence-informed revision

What I learned

The project reinforced that simulation works best when it is not treated as a dramatic add-on. The cases, criteria, feedback, LMS flow and evaluation all need to serve the same judgement task. My role was to keep that task visible while coordinating substantial clinical, production and technical detail.
